"The webinar went really well: 250 registrants, 40% attendance" is a sentence marketing teams say a lot. It's usually followed, a few weeks later, by sales quietly asking why none of those 100 attendees turned into anything.

Attendance and pipeline are not the same metric, and a webinar can succeed on the first while failing completely on the second. If that's happening to your program, the cause is almost always one of four things.

## 1. You're optimising registration for volume, not fit

A webinar promoted broadly, with a wide email send, general LinkedIn boosting, and a generic "free webinar" angle, pulls a wide audience. Some of that audience is your ICP. A lot of it isn't: students, competitors, people collecting webinar certificates, or simply people mildly curious about the topic with no buying context.

**The fix:** be more specific in your promotion, even if it shrinks the registration number. A subject line and topic angle aimed squarely at "marketing leaders at 50 to 500 person SaaS companies evaluating X" will pull fewer registrants than a broad "5 trends in marketing" angle, and it will convert dramatically better, because the audience that shows up already has the context that makes them a real prospect. As an example, a session titled "5 Trends in Marketing Automation for 2027" might pull 300 registrants from a broad send, while a session titled "How Marketing Teams Under 50,000 Contacts Are Cutting Automation Costs Without Losing Personalisation" might pull 90, and still convert at three or four times the rate, simply because everyone who registered already has the specific context that makes the topic relevant to a decision they're actively making.

## 2. The session informs but doesn't move anyone toward a decision

A genuinely excellent, informative webinar can still generate zero pipeline if it never gives the audience a reason to act now rather than "someday." If your content is structured as "here's everything about this topic," attendees leave better educated and no closer to a decision. A session that spends 30 minutes explaining a concept and five minutes on next steps will inform. A session that spends five minutes framing the cost of inaction, twenty minutes showing the mechanism through a real example, and five minutes on a named next step will move people, even when it covers the same core topic.

**The fix:** structure the session around a specific decision point (see [our guide on running pipeline-generating webinars](/blog/b2b-webinar-generate-pipeline)), and make the next step concrete and named, not generic.

## 3. Follow-up stops at the recording link

If your entire post-webinar process is "send the recording," you're relying on the attendee to independently decide to become a lead. Almost none will. The follow-up sequence is where a large share of actual conversion happens: segmented by engagement, with a specific next step, and ideally including direct outreach from sales for the highest-fit attendees. It's also the step most commonly skipped or minimised, because the "main event" already happened and momentum has moved on to the next thing. A team that segments follow-up into attendees who asked a question, attendees who stayed to the end, and everyone else, and only has sales call the first group directly, will typically see a meaningfully higher response rate than a team sending one identical email to the whole list.

## 4. Sales and marketing disagree on what a qualified lead looks like

Sometimes the pipeline is there, but it's invisible because sales isn't following up on webinar attendees the same way they follow up on other lead sources. Often nobody agreed in advance on what "webinar-qualified" means, so webinar leads never get routed into the same process as other inbound.

**The fix:** before your next session, agree explicitly on what attendee behaviour (title, company size, engagement level) triggers direct sales follow-up, and what the expected response time is. Webinar leads go cold fast. Same-week follow-up matters more here than almost any other channel, because the context is fresh in the attendee's mind. In practice this agreement can be as simple as a shared one page note: the title and company size band that qualifies automatically, plus any engagement signal, a question asked or a late-stage link clicked, that upgrades a borderline attendee into the same lane as other inbound leads.

## A worked example

Picture two versions of the same webinar. Version one promotes broadly, pulls 300 registrants, gets 35% attendance, sends a single generic thank you email, and produces two sales conversations a month later. Version two promotes to a narrower list matched to the ICP, pulls 90 registrants, gets 45% attendance because the audience is more invested, structures the session around a specific decision, and follows up with a segmented sequence that flags the eight most engaged attendees to sales within 48 hours. Version two produces far fewer total leads and meaningfully more real pipeline, because almost every step between registration and a sales conversation was designed around fit and follow-through rather than volume. Registrant count is the only metric where version one wins, and it's also the only one that doesn't actually matter.

## The audit worth running this quarter

Pull your last four to six webinars and, for each, look at the registration source and audience fit, whether the session had a specific CTA beyond "learn more," what the follow-up sequence actually contained, and whether sales followed up and how. Almost every team doing this audit for the first time finds the leak isn't attendance. It's one of the three steps after it.
