At some point, every marketing team running regular webinars has the same conversation: do we hire someone for this, or keep patching it together with whoever has time?

The instinct is usually to hire. It feels like the "proper" solution — a dedicated person, full control, no dependency on an outside partner. But the actual cost comparison rarely gets made honestly, because most of the cost of an in-house hire is hidden outside the salary line.

Here's the fuller picture.

## The visible cost: salary

A dedicated events or webinar specialist in a B2B marketing team typically commands a meaningful full-time salary once you include employer costs — National Insurance, pension contributions, benefits, and equipment. That's before any tools.

## The costs that don't show up in the job ad

**Hiring time.** Sourcing, screening, and interviewing for a specialised role usually takes 6–10 weeks, during which your webinar program either stalls or continues being run by someone doing it as a side task.

**Ramp-up.** A new hire — even a good one — needs 60–90 days to learn your product, your audience, your existing tools, and your team's way of working before they're operating at full speed. That's a quarter where output is below what you're paying for.

**Tool stack.** Webinar platform, registration page builder, email automation, design tools, video editing software — most of this gets bought again even if adjacent tools already exist elsewhere in the business, because access, seats, or workflows don't line up.

**Management overhead.** Someone needs to set direction, review output, and cover for them when they're on leave or when volume spikes around a launch. That's a real, if partial, cost on a manager's time — rarely accounted for when the hiring decision gets made.

**Single point of failure.** If your one events person leaves, is out sick during a launch week, or simply hasn't run the specific format you now need (say, a multi-day virtual summit), there's no backup. Momentum on a monthly webinar program is hard to rebuild after a two-month gap.

## What outsourcing changes

Working with a dedicated events partner doesn't remove cost — it restructures it. Instead of a fixed salary plus hidden overhead, you get:

- **No hiring or ramp-up time** — delivery starts from the first sessions, not after a quarter of onboarding.
- **No single point of failure** — a team, not one person, is behind delivery.
- **Tools included** — platform, production, and editing tools are part of the service rather than a separate procurement process.
- **Delivery scales with a monthly plan**, not a headcount decision — going from one webinar a month to two doesn't require a second hire.

The trade-off is real too: you don't get a dedicated person embedded in your team full-time, and you're relying on someone else's process rather than building your own institutional muscle. For teams planning to run a large internal events function long-term — conferences, in-person activations, a full content studio — building in-house eventually makes sense.

But for the specific job of "we want consistent, well-run webinars without a hiring cycle," outsourcing is very often the cheaper and faster path to the same outcome, particularly in the first 12–18 months.

## The question worth asking before either decision

Not "can we afford to hire" or "can we afford an agency" — but **how many events per month do we actually need to hit our pipeline goals, sustainably, for the next year?** That number tells you whether you need a person, a partner, or just a better process with the team you already have.
